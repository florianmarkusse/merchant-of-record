## The key structural point: who the "importer" is

For DDP B2C parcels, the express carrier files the entry, as an informal Type 11
entry or a formal entry, now that Section 321 de minimis is gone. On that entry,
someone is the **importer of record (IOR)**. That can be us, as a non-resident
importer, or the buyer as consignee with the carrier acting as broker.

- **US CPSA:** "manufacturer" includes any person who imports (15 USC 2052(a)(11)).
  The certifying party for imported products is the importer (16 CFR 1110.7(a), as
  amended by the final rule of 08-Jan-2025). The rule lets the "importer" for mail
  and low-value shipments be the owner, purchaser, consignee or authorised
  broker [A: GDLSK, Covington, STR]. **If we are the IOR, we are the CPSA
  "manufacturer"**: we issue the certificate, eFile it, and owe the Section 15(b)
  defect reports and recalls. Leaving the buyer as consignee-IOR is legally
  murky for a DDP seller, and it does not remove our Textile Act and FTC Act
  liability as seller.
- **US Textile, Wool and Fur Acts:** liability falls on anyone who manufactures,
  imports, sells, advertises or delivers the product. "An importer is legally
  responsible for the proper labeling of imported textile, wool and fur
  products" [P: FTC, Threading Your Way]. As the seller we are liable whatever
  the IOR choice.
- **Canada CCPSA:** s.7 binds the "manufacturer or importer" and s.8 binds any
  seller who knows of the danger. The s.14(3) written incident report falls on
  the manufacturer, **or the importer if the manufacturer is outside Canada**
  [P: CCPSA]. When a foreign seller ships DDP to a Canadian consumer, the
  importer is whoever imports into Canada. In practice that is us, as the
  non-resident importer the carrier declares. **UNVERIFIED** whether Health
  Canada would treat the consumer as importer. Assume it is us.
- **Canada Textile Labelling Act:** "dealer" includes "a person who is engaged
  in the business of importing or selling any textile fibre product" [P:
  Competition Bureau guide]. That is us.

**Recommendation:** decide to act as IOR (US non-resident IOR, needing a CBP
Form 5106 and probably a continuous bond, plus a Canadian non-resident importer
with a CBSA BN/RM account) and own these duties. The duties follow us either
way, and owning the IOR role keeps the CPSC eFiling chain coherent.

## United States

### 1. CPSC: GCC and 16 CFR 1610 flammability for adult apparel

- **Requirement:** every "clothing textile" must meet 16 CFR 1610, the
  Flammable Fabrics Act standard for clothing. Under CPSA s.14(a)(1) the
  importer must issue a GCC based on a test or a reasonable testing programme.
  Exempt from *testing* under 1610.1(d): (d)(1) plain-surface fabrics of
  2.6 oz/yd² or more regardless of fibre; (d)(2) fabrics made entirely of
  acrylic, modacrylic, nylon, olefin, polyester or wool, regardless of weight.
  Since 25-Mar-2016 CPSC's enforcement-discretion policy says it will not pursue
  a missing GCC for adult apparel that is exempt from testing under 1610.1(d)
  [P: CPSC 2016 policy, 81 FR 12594]. Non-exempt adult apparel still needs
  a GCC: raised-surface fabrics (fleece, chenille, terry, sherpa), lightweight
  cotton, rayon or silk under 2.6 oz/yd², and blends with elastane under that
  weight. Excluded from 1610 entirely: hats, gloves, footwear and interlining
  fabrics (1610.1(c)) [N: Swap].
- **High-risk SKUs:** sheer silk or rayon scarves and chiffon tops, rayon
  chenille, cotton fleece and terry. These are the classic Class 3 (rapid,
  intense burning) failures, and Class 3 is banned. Scarves are clothing
  textiles and are covered.
- **Cross-border B2C:** applies. The Flammable Fabrics Act prohibits
  importing non-compliant articles, whatever the channel.
- **Who:** the importer issues the certificate (us, if IOR). The VN merchant
  supplies fabric specs or test reports.
- **Data per SKU:** fibre composition, fabric weight, surface type, exemption
  basis or a 16 CFR 1610 test report (Class 1, from any competent lab; adult
  apparel does not need a third-party lab).

### 2. CPSC eFiling of certificates (mandatory 08-Jul-2026)

- **Rule:** final rule approved 18-Dec-2024, published 08-Jan-2025 (amending 16 CFR
  1110). Certificate data must be filed electronically with CBP at entry, through
  the ACE PGA Message Set, either in full or as a reference to a CPSC Product
  Registry entry. Effective 08-Jul-2026; FTZ entries 08-Jan-2027 [P: CPSC press
  release; A: Covington, STR, GDLSK].
- **Low-value / informal:** law firms said de minimis was not exempt: "any
  product requiring certification must have an eFiled certificate, regardless
  of the value" [A: GDLSK]. Since the US de minimis ended, our parcels go on
  Type 11 informal or formal entries, which carry PGA data, so filing applies.
  Express carriers (FedEx, UPS) now ask shippers for CPSC fields [N: FedEx
  CPSC FAQ, UPS].
- **Disclaims:** **Disclaim A** = not CPSC-regulated or no certificate needed.
  This covers footwear, bags, adult jewellery, belts and hats, which have no
  certificate requirement for adults. **Disclaim B** = enforcement discretion,
  e.g. adult apparel exempt under 1610.1(d) [N: Swap, UPS; A: GHY]. Filing
  a disclaim is described as optional but recommended to avoid holds [N: UL].
  The CBP clarification (CSMS #69382435) says ACE accepts entries without
  CPSC data if the PGA Message Set requirements are otherwise met, but "CPSC may
  review submitted data, reject CPSC data, or take enforcement action" [A: GHY,
  updated 30-Jul-2026].
- **Enforcement:** at launch CPSC indicated warnings rather than refusals, with
  escalation expected [N: Swap, updated 10-Oct-2026]. Missing data feeds CPSC
  risk scoring and can lead to exams or detention. I found no public data on
  post-launch detentions of parcels.
- **Per-SKU/variant data:** certifier ID or disclaim code, product ID, version
  ID, intended-use code (e.g. 130.006 = aged 13+, required with Disclaim B).
  For a full certificate: GTIN/SKU, citations (16 CFR 1610), manufacturer name and
  address, date and place of manufacture, date and place of testing, lab, records
  custodian. Registration in the Product Registry can take weeks [N: Swap].
- **System implication:** our catalogue needs a per-variant `cpsc_filing` value
  (certificate reference or disclaim code with use code). The carrier label or
  commercial-invoice integration must pass it through.

### 3. CPSIA for children's products (brief)

Applies only if a SKU is designed or intended mainly for children 12 and under.
Requirements: a Children's Product Certificate based on testing at a
CPSC-accepted third-party lab, total lead in substrate of 100 ppm or less and
in surface coatings of 90 ppm or less, phthalates (child-care articles and toys
only, so rarely apparel), permanent tracking labels, 16 CFR 1610 (no
enforcement discretion for kids), sleepwear rules (16 CFR 1615/1616),
drawstrings (16 CFR 1120.3(b): neck drawstrings in children's upper outerwear
sizes 2T–12, waist drawstrings 2T–16 are substantial product hazards), and
children's jewellery lead and ASTM F2923. eFiling is mandatory for children's
products. **Recommendation:** exclude kidswear at launch. The US fixed cost
(third-party lab per SKU) is high.

### 4. FTC Textile Act, Wool Act, Fur Act, online disclosure

- **Label contents:** generic fibre names with % by weight, country of origin
  (the origin must be on the front of the label; for garments with a neck,
  the label goes inside the centre of the neck), and the name or RN of the
  manufacturer or a dealer [P: FTC Threading]. Imported goods may show the
  foreign manufacturer's or distributor's name, or the name or RN of the
  importer, wholesaler or retailer (with the retailer's consent) [P]. **RNs are
  issued only to US-based businesses**, so a foreign company "should put its
  own name on the label" [P: FTC RN FAQ]. For us, either label with the VN
  manufacturer's name, which is easiest since the factory already does this,
  or with our legal name.
- **Coverage:** clothing (except hats and shoes), scarves, handkerchiefs,
  hosiery. Not footwear (except wool slippers under the Wool Rules), headwear
  (except wool hats under the Wool Rules), textiles in handbags or luggage, or
  belts (unless a fibre claim is made). Leather and trim are not covered; the
  Leather Guides (16 CFR 24) apply instead [P].
- **Online listings:** mail order includes internet sites. Each product
  description must state clearly and conspicuously "Made in U.S.A.",
  "imported" or both, and "a general statement ... that all products are ...
  imported is not adequate" [P]. If the page mentions fibre at all, the label's
  fibre information must appear minus the percentages [P]. **This duty is
  ours** because we publish the listing, as MoR and storefront operator.
  Merchants who run their own storefronts through our checkout share it.
- **Who:** anyone who manufactures, imports, sells or advertises. Retailers
  may not remove labels. Guaranties from suppliers are a defence [P].
- **Penalties:** each mislabelled item is a separate violation; civil penalties
  per violation (the FTC page states $53,088; adjusted yearly); mislabelled
  imports can be held by CBP and liable to liquidated damages [P].
- **Wool:** no tolerance if labelled "100% wool"; cashmere needs an average
  diameter of 19 microns or less [P].
- **Fur:** labels must show animal name, country of origin, and whether
  dyed or bleached. Real-fur trim mislabelled as faux is an FTC enforcement
  pattern. Dog and cat fur are banned (Dog and Cat Protection Act).
  **Recommendation:** no real fur.
- **Per-SKU:** fibre % (from the merchant's spec), origin, label identity,
  wool/cashmere proof, fur yes/no.

### 5. Care Labeling Rule, 16 CFR 423

Textile wearing apparel must carry a permanent care label with a reasonable
basis for the instructions. The definition excludes "footwear, gloves, hats or
other articles used exclusively to cover or protect the head or hands" [P:
16 CFR 423.1 via Cornell]. Manufacturers and importers owe it. The rule is
still in force; FTC reviews (2020 ANPR) have not repealed it. **UNVERIFIED**
whether any 2025–26 FTC action changed it. Online disclosure is not required.
Per-SKU: care instructions or symbols.

### 6. Country of origin marking, 19 USC 1304

Every article of foreign origin must be marked legibly, indelibly and
permanently with its English country name, in a conspicuous place, unless
excepted (the J-list, or an article that cannot be marked, which is then marked
on its container). Textiles follow the 19 CFR 102.21 origin rules: the country
where the fabric was formed or the garment wholly assembled. A VN-sewn garment
cut from Chinese fabric is usually "Made in Vietnam" for knit-to-shape or
assembled apparel, but check certain HTS headings (scarves, flat goods) that
take fabric origin. **UNVERIFIED for specific HTS.** Applies to all entries,
whatever their value. Missing marks mean exams and a 10% marking duty.
Per-SKU: origin, and for textiles the fabric origin.

### 7. California Prop 65

- **Requirement:** a "clear and reasonable" warning before exposing a
  Californian to a listed chemical above the safe-harbour level. For online
  sales, the warning must be given on the product (or its label) and
  "also" on the product display page, through a clearly marked hyperlink on
  that page, or prominently before checkout (27 CCR 25602(b), last amended
  26-Nov-2024, operative 01-Jan-2025) [P: CCR via Cornell].
- **Who:** 27 CCR 25600.2 puts the duty mainly on manufacturers, producers,
  packagers, importers, suppliers and distributors. Retailers are responsible
  when they are the importer, sell under their own label, received a warning
  notice and failed to post it, or knowingly introduced the chemical
  (UNVERIFIED wording; OEHHA pages would not render). **If we are IOR, we
  are the importer, and we are the retailer that posts the online warning.**
  The VN merchant is the manufacturer, but private enforcers sue whoever they
  can reach in the US market, which in practice means the seller.
- **10-employee threshold:** Prop 65 does not apply to businesses with fewer
  than 10 employees. As far as I know there is no carve-out for out-of-state
  or foreign sellers: enforcers routinely send 60-day notices to foreign
  online sellers. **UNVERIFIED:** whether employees of affiliates and
  employees outside California count (common reading: all employees, worldwide).
  If we have 10 or more employees we are covered; a small VN merchant may not
  be, which pushes the duty onto us.
- **Relevant chemicals in our categories:** lead (jewellery, PVC/vinyl
  handbags, zipper pulls, metal hardware), DEHP/DINP/DIDP/DBP (PVC bags,
  shoes, straps, prints), BPA (polycarbonate sunglasses), hexavalent chromium
  (chrome-tanned leather), cadmium (jewellery), PFOA/PFOS (water-repellent
  finishes), formaldehyde. Consent judgments set de facto limits (e.g. lead in
  handbags and fashion accessories, lead in jewellery). **UNVERIFIED:** the
  exact numbers. Typical are 90–300 ppm lead in accessible components and
  1,000 ppm DEHP.
- **Short-form warning amendments:** the 2024 amendment requires the chemical
  name in short-form warnings. Products labelled under the old short form
  get until **01-Jan-2028**, with sell-through for products made before then
  (**UNVERIFIED** dates).
- **Penalty:** up to $2,500 per day per violation, plus private-enforcer
  settlements. Mostly 60-day notices followed by settlement.
- **Practical:** either test or certify below the consent-judgment limits per
  material, or show a warning (on the product, or on the packing slip as
  "product label"? **UNVERIFIED** whether that suffices) AND on the
  product page for CA shipping addresses. Per-SKU: material list, test
  results for lead, phthalates and Cr(VI), and a warning flag plus the chemical
  name.

### 8. California metal-containing jewellery law (HSC 25214.1–25214.4.2)

From prior knowledge, **UNVERIFIED** this session: jewellery for adults must use
"Class 1" materials or meet lead limits by component. Electroplated metal under
6% lead, unplated metal under 1.5%, plastic or rubber under 0.02%, paint or
surface coatings under 0.06%, glass or crystal limits by weight. Children's
jewellery (aged 6 and under, or as defined) must be under 0.009% (90 ppm) lead
and under 0.03% (300 ppm) cadmium. "Jewellery" covers anklets, bracelets,
brooches, chains, earrings, necklaces, rings, hair accessories, charms and
pendants; belts and bags are not jewellery. Owed by the manufacturer, supplier
and seller. Other states (MN, IL, WA, MD, NY) restrict cadmium in children's
jewellery. Per-SKU: component materials and a lead test or supplier
certification.

### 9. State PFAS bans on apparel and textiles

All of this is **UNVERIFIED** this session; the state sites blocked fetches.
These are working assumptions from prior knowledge.

- **California AB 1817 (HSC 108970 ff.):** from 01-Jan-2025 no textile article
  (incl. apparel, handbags, accessories) may contain regulated PFAS. That means
  intentionally added PFAS, or total organic fluorine of 100 ppm or more, falling
  to 50 ppm from 01-Jan-2027. Outdoor apparel for severe wet conditions is exempt
  until 2028 with a "made with PFAS chemicals" disclosure. Manufacturers must
  give sellers a certificate of compliance. Possible 2025 amendments (SB 682)
  not checked.
- **New York:** apparel ban from 31-Dec-2023; outdoor apparel for severe wet
  conditions from 01-Jan-2025 (or with a disclosure).
- **Minnesota (Amara's Law):** intentionally added PFAS banned in clothing and
  other categories from 01-Jan-2025. Reporting of all products with
  intentionally added PFAS was due 01-Jul-2026, with extensions available.
- **Maine:** apparel/textile ban from 01-Jan-2026; the reporting duty was
  narrowed and moved to later dates.
- **Vermont:** apparel ban 01-Jan-2026. **Colorado:** apparel ban 01-Jan-2026
  (outdoor apparel 2028). **Washington:** Safer Products rule restricting PFAS in
  apparel and accessories from about 2027.
- **Who:** the "manufacturer", usually defined to include the importer or first
  domestic distributor when the maker is abroad. Several states also prohibit
  selling or distributing, which reaches us.
- **Practical:** our VN merchants rarely add PFAS except as water-repellent
  finishes on outerwear, bags and footwear. Require a written PFAS-free
  declaration per material; spot-test TOF on water-repellent SKUs; ban
  PFAS-finished SKUs from the US catalogue.

### 10. UFLPA and Tariff Act s.307 (forced labour)

- The UFLPA rebuttable presumption covers goods "mined, produced, or
  manufactured wholly or in part in the XUAR, or by an entity on the UFLPA
  Entity List". These are barred under 19 USC 1307 unless the importer shows
  "by clear and convincing evidence" that no forced labour was involved
  [P: CBP UFLPA page, modified 14-Sep-2026]. Section 307 also supports
  entity-specific Withhold Release Orders.
- **Relevance:** Vietnamese garment makers often use Chinese yarn and fabric,
  so cotton is a DHS high-priority sector. Vietnam is consistently among the
  top origin countries for UFLPA detentions by value. CBP uses isotopic testing
  on cotton [A, prior knowledge].
- **Small parcels:** UFLPA applies to all imports regardless of value. CBP
  has said it targets the de minimis stream; since de minimis ended, our
  parcels are ordinary entries. **UNVERIFIED** how often CBP detains
  single B2C parcels under UFLPA. In practice detentions hit bulk
  consolidations, and a lone parcel is more likely just refused or abandoned.
- **Documents CBP asks for (applicability or admissibility review):** supply
  chain map from raw cotton to finished good, with every party (ginner,
  spinner, weaver/knitter, dyer, garment maker); purchase orders, invoices,
  payment records, packing lists, bills of lading for each tier; production
  records; cotton origin certificates; affidavits. CBP publishes a "UFLPA
  Attachment to Notice of Detention" with examples [P].
- **Who:** the importer (us as IOR). Per-SKU: fibre origin by tier,
  especially cotton. Require a merchant declaration that no XUAR inputs and no
  Entity List suppliers were used, with document production on request. Screen
  merchants' suppliers against the UFLPA Entity List.

### 11. Lacey Act and USFWS

- **Lacey Act** plant-product declarations (PPQ Form 505) are required only for
  HTS codes in APHIS's phased schedule: wood articles, some chapters 44, 66, 94,
  95, 97. Cotton, linen and other textiles are exempt as common cultivars or
  not listed. Wooden or bamboo accessories (e.g. bamboo sunglasses, wooden
  jewellery) may fall in listed headings. **UNVERIFIED** per HTS. If listed,
  the importer files the genus, species and country of harvest.
- **USFWS:** imported "wildlife" products need a declaration (Form 3-177) at a
  designated port and, for commercial importers, a USFWS import/export licence.
  CITES species also need a CITES export permit from Vietnam. Exotic leathers
  (python, crocodile, lizard, stingray, ostrich) and feathers, shells, coral and
  horn are wildlife. Cow, sheep, goat and pig leather is not. **Recommendation:**
  exclude exotic leathers, feathers and shells at launch. Per-SKU:
  animal-material flag and species.

### 12. State fur bans

California AB 44 bans selling or manufacturing new fur products in California
from 01-Jan-2023. Fur excludes cowhide, deer, sheep and goat skin and shearling;
it includes rabbit and fox. Several cities have similar bans. Per-SKU: real fur
yes or no.

### 13. FTC Mail Order Rule (16 CFR 435)

The seller must have a reasonable basis for any shipping-time promise; with no
promise, it must ship within 30 days. On a delay, it must notify the buyer and
offer the option to cancel. Refunds go out promptly: 7 working days, or one
billing cycle for credit. Applies to us as seller to US consumers; the FTC
asserts jurisdiction over foreign sellers that target US buyers. System
implication: shipping estimates per lane must be data-backed, and a delay must
trigger the notice and option flow. Click-to-Cancel and negative-option rules
are irrelevant.

## Canada

### 14. Textile Labelling Act and Regulations

- "Dealer" includes a person "engaged in the business of importing or selling
  any textile fibre product" [P: Competition Bureau guide]. That is us.
- **Label:** fibre content in % by mass, in English and French, plus dealer
  identity: name and full postal address, or a **CA identification number,
  which is only for dealers in Canada** ($100) [P]. A foreign dealer uses name
  and address; whether a VN or SG/NL address suffices for an imported article is
  **UNVERIFIED**. The guide says importing articles without a disclosure label
  is prohibited [P].
- Country of origin is not required by the Textile Act unless an "imported"
  claim is made [P], but **CBSA requires it** (see 16).
- **Exempt:** footwear, belts and suspenders, handbags and luggage, straw or
  felt headwear [P]. Jewellery is not a textile.
- **Advertising:** fibre content in advertising is not mandatory; if given, the
  rules apply, though percentages may be omitted [P].
- Per-SKU: bilingual fibre content, dealer identity.

### 15. Canada Consumer Product Safety Act

- s.7: no manufacturer or importer may manufacture, import, advertise or sell
  a product that is a danger to human health or safety, or is recalled.
  s.8: no person may advertise or sell such a product knowingly. s.9: no
  misleading safety claims [P: CCPSA].
- s.13: everyone who manufactures, imports, advertises, sells or tests for
  commercial purposes keeps records of where they got the product and who
  they sold it to (retailers: from whom only). Records are kept for **6 years**
  at a Canadian place of business unless the Minister exempts [P]. **Issue:** we
  have no Canadian place of business. We would need an exemption, or a Canadian
  records location or agent (**UNVERIFIED** practice).
- s.14: on an incident, the manufacturer, importer or seller tells Health
  Canada and its supplier **within 2 days**. The manufacturer, **or the importer
  if the manufacturer is outside Canada**, files a written report **within 10
  days** [P]. As importer, both duties fall on us. We need an incident intake
  in support tooling.
- Per-SKU: supplier and batch traceability.

### 16. CBSA country of origin marking

The Marking of Imported Goods Regulations apply to non-CUSMA goods, including
Vietnam, for Schedule I goods. These include boots, shoes and slippers;
leather gloves; handbags and purses; hats; knitted garments; and "apparel made
substantially or wholly of natural or synthetic textile fibres". Marking must be
in English or French and show the country where the goods were "substantially
manufactured" [P: CBSA D11-3-1]. Jewellery and belts are not obviously listed
(**UNVERIFIED**).

### 17. Textile Flammability Regulations (SOR/2016-194)

Apply to all textile products except the listed exclusions (children's
sleepwear up to 14X, which has its own rules, toys, cribs, tents, mattresses,
and others). Flame-spread time must exceed **3.5 s** for plain surfaces and
**4 s** for raised-fibre surfaces that ignite or fuse the base fibres, under
CAN/CGSB-4.2 No. 27.5. Last amended 20-Nov-2024 [P]. No fibre or weight
exemption list like the US one appears in the text, so the importer needs a
basis for compliance; in practice US 1610 Class 1 results are commonly relied
on (**UNVERIFIED** whether Health Canada accepts them). Owed by the
manufacturer or importer.

### 18. Lead, children's jewellery, surface coatings

The Children's Jewellery Regulations limit lead and cadmium; the Consumer
Products Containing Lead Regulations (SOR/2018-83) limit total lead to 90 mg/kg
in mouthed products and some children's products; the Surface Coating
Materials Regulations limit lead in coatings to 90 mg/kg. For adult fashion
jewellery there is no specific Canadian lead limit, but the s.7 general
prohibition applies. All **UNVERIFIED** this session. Only relevant to children's
SKUs and coated products.

### 19. Consumer Packaging and Labelling Act

Applies to "prepackaged" consumer products: net quantity, product identity,
dealer identity, bilingual. Single garments are generally not prepackaged in
the CPLA sense; multipacks of socks may need a count declaration.
**UNVERIFIED.** Low priority.

### 20. Quebec Charter of the French Language

Product inscriptions, packaging and documents (warranty, instructions) must be
in French, and the website must be available in French for Quebec buyers.
Bill 96 tightened product-labelling and trademark rules from 01-Jun-2025.
Application to foreign online sellers is asserted by the OQLF; enforcement
against foreign sellers is weak. **UNVERIFIED**, and the consumer-law
researcher may cover it.

### 21. Forced labour: S-211 report and Customs Tariff 9897.00.00

- **S-211 (Fighting Against Forced Labour and Child Labour in Supply Chains
  Act):** reports are due to Public Safety by **31 May** each year. It applies
  to an "entity" listed in Canada, or with a place of business, business or
  assets in Canada, that meets 2 of 3 criteria in one of its last two financial
  years: **C$20M assets, C$40M revenue, 250 employees**. Covered activities are
  producing goods anywhere, importing goods made outside Canada, or controlling
  an entity that does [P: Public Safety]. Selling DDP to Canada is arguably
  "doing business in Canada", but at our size we fall below the thresholds.
  Revisit when revenue approaches C$40M.
- **Customs Tariff item 9897.00.00** has prohibited goods made by forced labour
  since 01-Jul-2020, with child labour added by S-211 from 01-Jan-2024. Enforcement
  has been minimal. A stronger import ban has been discussed (**UNVERIFIED**).
  The same traceability documents as for UFLPA cover it.

## Per-SKU data model (consolidated)

Collect from the merchant, under a signed warranty or guaranty:

1. Product category (apparel / footwear / bag / jewellery / belt / hat /
   scarf / sunglasses), age grade (adult only at launch).
2. Materials by component: fibre % by weight (generic names, EN and FR), fabric
   weight in oz/yd² or g/m², surface (plain / raised), leather type (genuine /
   split / bonded / imitation), real fur (yes/no + species), animal-derived
   exotic material (yes/no + species), plant-based material (wood/bamboo + genus).
3. Origin: country of manufacture; fabric country (for 19 CFR 102.21); raw
   fibre origin by tier for cotton (UFLPA).
4. Label artwork, or confirmation of label content: fibre, origin, identity
   (merchant name, or ours), care instructions, bilingual for Canada.
5. Flammability: 1610 exemption basis, or a test report (Class 1); a Canada
   27.5 basis.
6. CPSC eFiling value per variant: certificate reference (Product Registry),
   or Disclaim A or B plus intended-use code.
7. Chemical: lead (metal, plastic, coating components), phthalates (PVC), Cr(VI)
   (leather), BPA (polycarbonate), cadmium (jewellery), PFAS declaration (TOF for
   water-repellent items). Plus a Prop 65 warning flag and chemical name.
8. Manufacturer name and address, date and place of manufacture, batch or lot
   (CPSC certificate, CCPSA records).
9. Supplier declaration: no XUAR inputs, no Entity List suppliers; documents
   on request within N days.
